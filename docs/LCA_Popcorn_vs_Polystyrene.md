# Popcorn vs. Polystyrene — An LCA Example

## Overview

This document walks through a classic teaching example from the LCA course: comparing two materials used as protective packaging fill inside shipping boxes. One is polystyrene — the familiar white foam chips. The other is popcorn — actual popped corn, used as a biodegradable alternative.

The example is used right at the start of the course because it delivers a surprising result. It shows that our gut instincts about what is "green" can be wrong, and that how you choose to measure things can completely change your conclusion.

---

## Step 1 — Goal and Scope

### What are we comparing?

Both materials serve the same job: filling the empty space inside a cardboard box to protect fragile items during shipping. The question is: which material has a lower environmental impact?

### The functional unit

The functional unit is the precise definition of what the packaging fill is supposed to *do*. Getting this right turns out to be the most important decision in this whole example.

At first glance, it seems reasonable to compare the two materials by weight — by the kilogram. But think about what packaging fill is actually doing. It is not providing mass. It is filling **space**. You pour it into a box until the box is full, and you stop. The amount you need depends on the volume of the box, not on how much the material weighs.

So the correct functional unit is:

> "Filling 1 cubic metre of box space with protective packaging material"

This single choice — cubic metre instead of kilogram — is what makes this example so instructive.

---

## Step 2 — Qualitative Comparison

Before doing any maths, it helps to think through the strengths and weaknesses of each material in plain terms.

### Polystyrene (white foam chips)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Problems** | Never breaks down — sits in landfill for centuries |
| **Problems** | Made from oil, a non-renewable resource |
| **Advantages** | Protects items very well |
| **Advantages** | Traps a lot of air, so it is extremely light for the volume it fills |
| **Advantages** | Can be reused many times |

### Popcorn (popped corn as packaging fill)

| | |
|---|---|
| **Problems** | Crumbly — small bits fall off and need cleaning up |
| **Problems** | Uses food crops for a non-food purpose, which raises ethical questions |
| **Advantages** | Compostable and biodegradable — breaks down naturally |
| **Advantages** | Grown using solar energy (the corn plant is essentially a large, cheap solar panel) |
| **Advantages** | Made from a renewable resource |

Looking at this list, popcorn appears to be the clearly greener choice. It is natural, renewable, and biodegradable. Polystyrene is a fossil-fuel plastic that lasts forever. Case closed — or so it seems.

---

## Step 3 — The Quantitative Twist

### Comparing by weight (the wrong approach)

When researchers first ran the numbers, they compared the two materials **per kilogram**. On that basis, popcorn came out **3 to 4 times better** than polystyrene across most environmental impact categories. Great news for the natural option.

But then someone asked a crucial question: *is kilogram really the right unit here?*

### The density problem

Here is the issue. Polystyrene is an extraordinarily light, airy material. Popcorn is much denser. To fill the same box to the same level of protection, you need **4.6 times as much popcorn by weight** as polystyrene.

In other words:

> To fill 1 cubic metre of box space, you need roughly 1 unit of polystyrene — but 4.6 units of popcorn.

When you redo the comparison measuring **per cubic metre** (the correct functional unit), the environmental burden of popcorn is multiplied by 4.6. The result flips completely.

### Comparing by volume (the right approach)

| Material | Impact per kg | Density factor | Impact per m³ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Polystyrene | Higher | 1× | Comparable |
| Popcorn | 3–4× lower per kg | 4.6× heavier | Comparable or worse |

What looked like a clear win for popcorn on a per-kilogram basis becomes much less clear — or even a disadvantage — when measured per cubic metre.

---

## Step 4 — Interpretation

### Key lessons from this example

**1. "Natural" is not the same as "environmentally friendly."**
Popcorn *feels* green because it is biodegradable and grown from a plant. But once you account for how much of it you actually need, the environmental advantage shrinks or disappears. The material's origin (plant vs. oil) is not the whole story.

**2. The functional unit is the most important decision in any LCA.**
Choosing kilogram instead of cubic metre gave a completely misleading answer. The right functional unit must reflect what the product or material is actually *doing* — in this case, filling space, not providing mass.

**3. Density matters more than you might expect.**
Because polystyrene is so light and fluffy, a tiny mass of it fills an enormous volume. That physical efficiency has real environmental significance. A material that looks wasteful because it is made of plastic can, in practice, require far fewer resources per unit of useful work done.

**4. LCA helps us go beyond biased intuition.**
Our instinct is to prefer things that are natural, biodegradable, or plant-based. LCA forces us to slow down and check whether that instinct holds up under measurement. Sometimes it does. Sometimes, as here, it does not — or the answer is more complicated than it first appeared.

---

## Summary

| | Polystyrene | Popcorn |
|---|---|---|
| **Raw material** | Oil (non-renewable) | Corn (renewable) |
| **End of life** | Landfill, very persistent | Compostable, biodegradable |
| **Impact per kg** | Higher | 3–4× lower |
| **Density** | Very low (light and airy) | 4.6× heavier per volume |
| **Impact per m³** | Comparable | Comparable or higher |
| **Verdict** | Not as bad as it looks | Not as good as it looks |

The popcorn vs. polystyrene example is a reminder that in life cycle assessment, the question you ask — and the unit you use to ask it — shapes everything that follows.
