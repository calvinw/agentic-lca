In Life Cycle Assessment, or **LCA** (a way of measuring environmental impact across a product’s life), every study has a “receipt” too. That boundary is called the **system boundary**. Think of it as a fence around part of the supply chain.

Everything inside the fence is measured and counted. Everything outside the fence is ignored.

For this cotton fiber study, the fence includes:
- **Fertilizer production**
- **Cotton farming**

Looking at that boundary, what cotton-related steps might happen **after cotton farming** that are not being counted here?

**Student:** what else is impotant about system boundary

**AI:** The most important thing is that the **system boundary controls what the final number means**.

If the boundary is narrow, the result will usually look smaller. If the boundary is wider, the result will usually look larger because more steps are being counted.

For cotton fiber, this study counts:
- Making synthetic fertilizer
- Growing and ginning the cotton fiber

It does **not** count:
- Spinning cotton into yarn
- Weaving or knitting fabric
- Dyeing
- Sewing a garment
- Shipping to stores
- Customer washing and drying
- Disposal or recycling

That matters because two companies could both say “our cotton has a low footprint,” but they might not be measuring the same thing. One company might only count the farm. Another might count farm, fertilizer, textile processing, transport, and disposal.

So the first question a buyer, brand manager, or sustainability team should ask is:

**“What is inside the boundary, and what is outside it?”**

That question protects you from comparing two numbers that look similar but are actually measuring different parts of the product’s life.
