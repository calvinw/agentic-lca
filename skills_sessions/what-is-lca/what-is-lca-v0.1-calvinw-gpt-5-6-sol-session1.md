**AI:** Imagine a cotton T-shirt and a polyester T-shirt are on your desk. One colleague says cotton is more sustainable because it is natural; another says polyester is better because it lasts longer.

Who do you think is right—and how could we fairly find out?

**Student:** dont we have to find a common way to compare them

**AI:** Exactly—that instinct is the beginning of the LCA mindset. We need a common, fair basis for comparison, such as “providing one wearable T-shirt for 100 wears,” and then examine the same environmental effects across both products.

LCA stands for Life Cycle Assessment. Think of it as a receipt showing a product’s hidden environmental costs—CO₂ emissions, water use, and pollution—from raw materials through production, use, and disposal.

Which impacts would you want that comparison to include?

**Student:** all of those and maybe more

**AI:** Exactly. A fair comparison might include climate emissions, water use, land use, energy demand, chemical pollution, and waste—because a shirt can perform well in one area but poorly in another.

For fashion buyers and designers, LCA helps replace a vague “sustainable” hangtag with evidence and reveals where improvements would matter most. Why might comparing only carbon emissions give you a misleading answer?

**Student:** since there are other considerations

**AI:** Exactly. A cotton shirt might have lower carbon emissions but require much more water, while a polyester shirt might use less water but depend on fossil fuels and release microfibers. LCA keeps us from calling something “better” after looking at only one environmental issue.

The first phase is **goal and scope**: decide why you are comparing the shirts and draw a fence around what the study will include. Where do you think that fence should end—at the factory, after customer use, or at disposal?
