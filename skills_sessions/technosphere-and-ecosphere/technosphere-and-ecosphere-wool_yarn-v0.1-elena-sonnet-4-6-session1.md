**AI:** Imagine a busy town that has a wall around it. Inside the wall: houses, factories, shops, roads — everything built and run by people. Outside the wall: fields, rivers, the sky, the forest — nature, exactly as it would be without humans.

Now imagine a factory inside the wall burns coal to run its machines. Two things happen: first, a truck drives through the town and delivers the coal (that coal never leaves the wall — it moves from one part of the human world to another). Second, the chimney sends smoke up into the sky above the wall — and that smoke drifts out beyond the wall into the fields and the river.

**Here is the question: which of those two things — the coal truck or the chimney smoke — actually crosses the wall and reaches the natural world outside?**

**Student:** chimney smoke

**AI:** Exactly — the coal truck stayed inside the town the whole time. It moved from a coal yard to a factory, but it never left the human-made world. The chimney smoke is the one that crossed the wall and drifted out into the fields and the river. That crossing is where environmental harm begins.

In Life Cycle Assessment, researchers use two names for these two worlds. The human-made world — everything inside the wall, like factories, farms, mills, and trucks — is called the **technosphere** (from "techno", meaning made by people). The natural world outside the wall — air, water, soil, living ecosystems — is called the **ecosphere** (from "eco", meaning the living environment).

The most important idea in LCA is this: environmental harm only happens when something **crosses the boundary** between the two. Emissions released into the ecosphere (like CO₂ into the air) and resources pulled out of the ecosphere (like water from a river) are the flows that cause impact. Everything that moves around *inside* the technosphere — a bag of raw wool travelling from a farm to a mill, say — stays inside the wall and does not directly harm the natural world.

**Inside the wall — the technosphere:**
The two boxes in this diagram are the **processes** — the human activities that make the yarn. In this study that means **Sheep farming** and **Wool yarn production**. Also inside the wall is the **intermediate product** flowing between those two boxes: **Raw wool** passing from the farm to the mill. That flow stays entirely inside the human-made world. It does not touch nature.

**Crossing the wall — into and out of the ecosphere:**
Two types of flows cross the boundary in this study:
- **Emissions going *out* into nature:** Carbon dioxide and Methane, both released to air. These are substances the processes produce and discharge into the natural world.
- **Resources coming *in* from nature:** Water, drawn from a natural water source to run the scouring and spinning operations.

In LCA, these crossings are called **elementary flows** — elementary because they are the basic exchanges between the human world and the natural world. They are the only flows that score as environmental impact, because they are the only ones that actually reach the ecosphere.

**Looking at those two emissions — Carbon dioxide and Methane — which one do you think causes more harm to the climate? Take a guess — there is no wrong answer here.**

**Student:** carbon dioxide

**AI:** That is a very reasonable guess — carbon dioxide is the gas we hear about most often in climate discussions, so it feels like the obvious answer. And this study does emit more CO₂ by weight than methane: 2.5 kg of CO₂ compared to 0.4 kg of methane from the sheep farm.

But here is the surprising part: **methane is actually the bigger climate problem in this study.** Scientists have measured how powerfully each gas traps heat, and it turns out that one kilogram of methane traps about **28 times** as much heat as one kilogram of CO₂. That methane comes from the sheep themselves — it is released during digestion, a process called enteric fermentation. The sheep burp and exhale it constantly, and every kilogram crosses the wall from the technosphere straight into the ecosphere.

So here is the what-if question: if the sheep farm *stopped emitting methane entirely* — same coal burned, same water used, same CO₂ released — but the methane crossing disappeared completely, how much of the total climate impact do you think would vanish? **More than half, or less than half?**

**Student:** more than half

**AI:** You are exactly right — and the actual number is striking. Once you apply that 28× multiplier to the methane, the maths looks like this:

- Methane from the sheep farm: 0.44 kg × 28 = **12.3 kg CO₂ equivalent**
- Carbon dioxide from the farm: **0.55 kg CO₂ equivalent**
- Carbon dioxide from the mill: **2.0 kg CO₂ equivalent**
- **Total: roughly 14.9 kg CO₂ equivalent per kilogram of wool yarn**

The methane alone accounts for about **83% of the total climate impact** — even though it looked like the smaller number on the page. Remove it, and nearly the entire footprint disappears. This is exactly why the ecosphere crossing matters more than the raw weight: a small amount of a potent gas crossing the wall can dwarf everything else in the study.

This has a direct lesson for anyone buying or sourcing wool. A supplier who focuses on reducing energy use at the mill — cutting the CO₂ from scouring and spinning — is tackling only 14% of the problem. The real hotspot is the sheep farm, and the real culprit is a gas that comes from the animals themselves. That is not easy to fix, but knowing *where* the crossing happens is the only way to know *where* to act.

The technosphere-ecosphere boundary is the reason LCA studies track **elementary flows** — that is the official name for the crossings we have been talking about. The next natural step is understanding how those flows get converted into impact scores — a process called **characterisation**, which is where that 28× multiplier actually comes from.
