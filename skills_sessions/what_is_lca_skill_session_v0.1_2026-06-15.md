**Skill version:** 0.1  
**Date:** 2026-06-15

**Skill:** Here is a question fashion buyers face all the time:

A cotton T-shirt and a polyester T-shirt are sitting on a desk in front of you.

One colleague says the cotton one is more sustainable because it is natural. Another colleague says the polyester one is more sustainable because it lasts longer.

Who is right, and how would you actually find out?

**Student:** which one do you think it is more sustainable? cotton vs polyester polyester last longer but cotton done

**Skill:** That is a good answer, and you are thinking about the right tradeoff.

Cotton often feels "more sustainable" because it is natural and can biodegrade more easily. But polyester can sometimes look better in a study if it lasts much longer, needs less water to produce, or replaces several cotton shirts over time.

This is exactly why **LCA**, which stands for **Life Cycle Assessment**, exists. LCA is a method for adding up the environmental impacts of a product across its life, from raw materials to manufacturing, use, and sometimes disposal. Think of it like a receipt that shows the hidden environmental costs of a product, not just the price tag.

So the honest answer is: **we cannot know from the material name alone**. We need to compare things like water use, energy use, carbon emissions, washing, durability, and what happens at the end of the product's life.

For someone in fashion or retail, this matters because "sustainable" is not enough by itself. LCA helps a buyer, designer, or brand manager ask: **sustainable compared to what, measured how, and over which part of the product's life?**

Would you like me to walk you through the four basic steps of an LCA study?

**Student:** yes

**Skill:** An LCA study has **four basic steps**. Think of them like planning, filling in a spreadsheet, calculating the scores, and then making a business decision.

1. **Goal and scope**

This means deciding **why you are doing the study** and **what you are counting**.

For example: are we comparing one cotton T-shirt and one polyester T-shirt? Are we counting only production, or also washing, wearing, and disposal?

2. **Inventory**

This means collecting the data.

It is like filling in a detailed spreadsheet: how much cotton, polyester, water, electricity, dye, transport, and packaging are used at each step.

3. **Impact assessment**

This means turning the raw data into environmental scores.

For example, the study converts emissions into categories like climate change impact, water use, air pollution, or chemical pollution.

4. **Interpretation**

This means reading the results and asking, "What does this mean for the business decision?"

For example, if cotton has a bigger water impact but polyester creates more plastic pollution, the answer may depend on what problem the company is trying hardest to reduce.

Which of those four steps do you think would be hardest to do in real life?

**Student:** 2

**Skill:** Exactly. **Inventory** is often the hardest step.

That is because it requires real data from many places in the supply chain. For a T-shirt, you might need information from the cotton farm, the polyester supplier, the spinning mill, the dye house, the sewing factory, the shipping company, the store, and even the customer's laundry habits.

Think of it like trying to build a full cost sheet for a product, but instead of only tracking dollars, you are tracking water, electricity, chemicals, fuel, waste, and emissions. If one supplier does not know or will not share their numbers, the study becomes less certain.

That is why LCA is useful, but also why we have to read LCA results carefully. The numbers are only as good as the information that went into them.

Every other skill in this course zooms into one part of that process. The next natural step is **goal and scope**, which is where we decide what the study is trying to answer and what gets counted.

1. **LCA** means Life Cycle Assessment.
2. It measures hidden environmental impacts across a product’s life.
3. It helps fashion and retail teams avoid vague sustainability claims.
4. The four main steps are **goal and scope**, **inventory**, **impact assessment**, and **interpretation**.
