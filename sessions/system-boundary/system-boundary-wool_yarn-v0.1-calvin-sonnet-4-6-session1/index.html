    Student: taxi parking, it would not be accurate

    AI: Exactly — the receipt only covers what happened inside that restaurant. Everything else, no matter how real and costly it was, simply does not appear on it.

    In Life Cycle Assessment, every study has a "receipt" too — a defined set of steps it covers and a defined set it does not. That boundary is called the system boundary. Think of it as a fence drawn around part of the supply chain. Everything inside the fence is measured and counted. Everything outside the fence is ignored — even if it causes real environmental harm.

    The system boundary is not a flaw — it is a deliberate choice. But it means the number you get at the end of a study only tells you about what was inside the fence.

    Let's look at a real example — a study of Wool Yarn. The goal is to calculate the climate impact of producing 1 kg of wool yarn, tracing the supply chain from sheep farming through to the spinning mill.

    Here is the supply chain diagram for this study:

    Each box in this diagram is a step the study decided to include. The fence — the system boundary — runs around both of these boxes. Any step that does not appear is outside the fence and was not counted.

    Looking at the diagram, can you spot any steps you might expect to be there that are not shown? For example — is there anything that happens to the yarn after it leaves the spinning mill that might also cause environmental impact?

    Student: yes, it gets washed or used

    AI: You are asking exactly the right thing — and yes, you've spotted something real.

    After the yarn leaves the spinning mill, it gets transported to a garment factory, knitted or woven into a sweater or blanket, shipped to a store, bought by a customer, washed many times at home, and eventually thrown away or donated. Every single one of those steps uses energy and causes some environmental impact — but none of them appear in this diagram, because they are all outside the fence.

    This study's boundary stops the moment the finished yarn leaves the mill. That is a common choice called cradle to gate — it covers everything from the sheep farm (the "cradle") up to the exit of the last factory (the "gate"), and nothing after.

    Here is the what-if question: what if the boundary had also included the energy used to transport the yarn to a garment factory, and then the garment factory itself — the cutting, sewing, and finishing of a finished sweater? Do you think the total carbon footprint number would go up, go down, or stay the same — and why?

    Student: it would go up clearly

    AI: Exactly right — and that is the single most important thing to understand about system boundaries.

    Every time you widen the fence to include more steps, the number goes up — because you are counting more real activity that causes real emissions. A narrow boundary will always produce a smaller number than a wide one, even if they are studying the same product.

    This matters enormously in a business context. When a wool supplier hands you an LCA result showing a low carbon footprint, the system boundary is the first thing to check — not the number. Two suppliers can report very different footprints for the same material simply because one drew a wider fence than the other. Neither number is necessarily wrong, but they are not the same thing, and you cannot compare them directly.

    The system boundary is one decision inside a bigger set called goal and scope — the opening chapter of every LCA study, where researchers decide why the study is being done, who it is for, and exactly where the fence gets drawn. Would you like to explore how system boundary fits into that bigger goal-and-scope picture, or is there anything about the boundary concept you'd like to dig into first?

    Student: So do people argue over the system boundary

    AI: This is a perfectly normal question — and yes, absolutely they do!

    System boundary disagreements are actually one of the most common arguments in sustainability reporting. A wool producer might publish a cradle-to-gate number that stops at the mill gate, while an environmental group might argue that the study should have gone all the way to end-of-life disposal. Both are using real data — but because the fences are drawn in different places, they get very different numbers, and each side can accuse the other of cherry-picking.

    This is also where greenwashing can happen — not necessarily through lying, but through choosing a conveniently narrow boundary and not being upfront about it. A brand might advertise "our wool sweater has a footprint of only 2 kg CO₂" — but if that number only covers the final spinning mill and nothing else, it leaves out the sheep farm, which in our wool yarn example is responsible for roughly 86% of the total impact.

    So the practical skill for someone in fashion buying or brand management is not to memorise carbon numbers — it is to always ask: "What is inside the fence, and what did you leave out?"
